\section{Monte Carlo Validation of Two-Stage Estimation}
\label{sec:simulation}

To assess the finite-sample performance of the two-stage estimation procedure described in the main text, we conduct a Monte Carlo simulation with a known data-generating process. The exercise compares three approaches to recovering the structural parameters and marginal effects of a network-formation model with two-sided fixed effects.

\paragraph{Data-generating process.} Each replication generates a network of PCPs and specialists assigned to HRR-year cells. We draw PCP fixed effects $\alpha_i$ and specialist fixed effects $\alpha_j$ that are correlated with the covariates, so that omitting specialist fixed effects produces genuine omitted-variable bias rather than merely adding noise. Specifically, physicians closer to the center of the HRR receive higher fixed effects, and male specialists receive higher $\alpha_j$. The referral outcome $y_{ij}$ is drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with probability $\text{logit}^{-1}(x_{ij}'\beta + \alpha_i + \alpha_j)$, where the true parameters are $\beta_{\text{practice}} = 2.5$, $\beta_{\text{gender}} = 0.5$, and $\beta_{\text{distance}} = -0.1$.

\paragraph{Estimators.} We compare three estimators in each replication. (1) The true $\beta$ and marginal effects computed directly from the known DGP. (2) A naive conditional logit with PCP fixed effects only, analogous to \cite{zeltzer2020}. (3) Our two-stage procedure, in which the first stage estimates $\beta$ via the Jochmans quadruple-based conditional logit and the second stage recovers the PCP and specialist fixed effects. Every specialist in the simulation is referred to by some PCP, so the separation that motivates estimating the specialist effects on the full sample in the main text does not arise, and the fixed effects are recovered together.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:sim-all} presents results across three scenarios that vary the degree of fixed-effect heterogeneity and covariate correlation. In the baseline scenario (Panel A), the naive estimator attenuates $\beta_{\text{practice}}$ by approximately 13\% and $\beta_{\text{gender}}$ by 12\%, while the two-stage procedure recovers both parameters to within 1\% of the true values. Under stronger covariate correlation (Panel B), attenuation in the naive estimator persists and the distance coefficient is overestimated by 23\%. Under both higher correlation and larger fixed-effect variance (Panel C), the naive estimator attenuates $\beta_{\text{practice}}$ by 33\% and $\beta_{\text{gender}}$ by 34\%, while the two-stage estimates remain within 1\% of the truth.

Marginal effects show smaller differences across estimators than the $\beta$ coefficients, because the naive estimator's PCP fixed effects partially compensate for the omitted specialist heterogeneity when computing predicted probabilities. Nonetheless, the two-stage procedure consistently produces marginal effects closer to the true values, particularly for distance, where the naive estimator overstates the effect by up to 60\% under higher correlation.


\section{Estimation Sample and Fixed-Effect Recovery}
\label{sec:convergence}

\paragraph{First-stage identifying sample.} The Jochmans quadruple estimator identifies $\beta$ from discordant quadruples, namely pairs of movers who refer to two specialists in opposite directions. Because identification comes only from pairs in which referral choices vary, and referrals are concentrated among organizationally and geographically proximate specialists, the pairs entering the estimation are more likely to be same-practice and, in orthopedic surgery, geographically closer than the full choice set. Table~\ref{tab:quadsample} reports these comparisons. This pattern is inherent to any within-group estimator, which draws identification from observations that exhibit outcome variation, and it does not bias the estimated coefficients. The specialists and PCPs entering the estimation are otherwise similar to those excluded on quality, experience, and demographic composition, so the estimated relationships are not identified from an unrepresentative subpopulation of physicians. The one systematic difference among providers is that included PCPs and specialists have modestly higher patient volume, a mechanical consequence of the fact that forming a discordant quadruple requires observed referrals.

\paragraph{Second-stage fixed-effect recovery.} Holding $\hat{\beta}$ fixed as an offset, we recover the fixed effects in two steps. Because movers make only a handful of referrals each, most specialists are never referred to by any mover and their effects cannot be recovered from the mover sample alone. We therefore estimate the specialist effects on the full sample of referring PCPs, movers and non-movers alike, where a specialist referred to at least once is identified, and winsorize at $\pm 6$ log-odds the small number of effects that remain perfectly separated even in the full sample. Holding these specialist effects fixed, we then recover the doctor effects on the mover sample, which converges cleanly. Two checks support this procedure. First, we test whether the full-sample specialist effects transfer to movers' referrals by entering them into the mover-referral model as a single regressor with coefficient $\lambda$, holding the structural index as an offset, recovering the doctor effects, and clustering the standard error by HRR. A coefficient $\lambda = 0$ would indicate that the effects carry no information for movers, and $\lambda = 1$ that they transfer at full strength, which is the common-specialist-effect assumption the model maintains. We estimate $\lambda = 0.99$ in orthopedic surgery, $0.95$ in dermatology, and $0.92$ in cardiology, each precisely estimated and far from zero ($p < 0.001$). We cannot reject $\lambda = 1$ in orthopedic surgery ($p = 0.79$) or dermatology ($p = 0.18$), and in cardiology $\lambda$ is modestly below one ($p = 0.003$), so the full-sample effects transfer to movers at close to, if not exactly, full strength. Second, we run the doctor-effect recovery at iteration counts of 10, 25, 50, 100, and 250 and compare the resulting average marginal effects.

Table~\ref{tab:convergence} presents the results for each specialty. In all three cases the average marginal effects, the model deviance, and the standard deviation of the recovered doctor effects all stabilize well before the maximum iteration count, confirming that the recovery is insensitive to the convergence criterion.

With the specialist effects estimated on the full sample, the separation that would drop most specialists from a mover-only recovery no longer arises, and the marginal effects are computed over the mover choice set as a whole rather than on a same-practice-tilted non-separated subsample. Because the choice set is dominated by pairs with negligible referral probability, we weight each pair by its predicted referral probability when averaging, so that the reported effect reflects pairs where a referral is otherwise plausible rather than being diluted over pairs that would almost never form.


\section{Mover Selection}
\label{sec:app-moverselect}

The mover design assumes that a physician's relocation is not driven by the referral opportunities available in the destination. We assess this in two ways. We first ask whether movers relocate to systematically different markets, and then whether movers are systematically different referrers before they move.

Table~\ref{tab:mover-markets} compares each mover's destination market to their origin market. Movers relocate to larger and more specialist-dense markets, with more specialists, more PCPs, and more specialists per PCP, as expected of physicians moving to new and often larger areas. On the dimensions that would bias our estimates, destinations instead resemble origins. The share of referrals made within a practice, the share of PCPs with a same-practice specialist available, and mean practice size are each slightly lower at the destination rather than higher. Movers do not relocate to markets that are more organizationally integrated or more oriented toward within-practice referral.

\section{Race Predictions}
\label{sec:race}

Physician race is not available in national administrative data. We impute race by combining three complementary classifiers and aggregating their predictions with a random-forest ensemble. This section summarizes the methods and their accuracy; full implementation details are available from the authors.

\paragraph{Individual classifiers.} We obtain race probabilities from three sources. First, NamePrism generates name-based probabilities using name embeddings trained on 57 million contact lists. Second, the WRU (``Who are You'') algorithm applies Bayes' rule to combine surname, first name, and geographic location with census-tract demographics, using the fully Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding (fBISG) methodology. Third, we obtain physician profile photos from publicly available Zocdoc profile pages and apply the DeepFace neural network to generate image-based race probabilities. We match Zocdoc profiles to our physician sample using first name, last name, and ZIP code. For consistency, we harmonize race categories across methods, consolidating API (Asian/Pacific Islander) and Asian into Asian, renaming Latino/Hispanic to Hispanic, and combining Indian and Middle Eastern into Other.

\paragraph{Ensemble prediction.} We aggregate these individual predictions using a random-forest classifier (Ranger package). The ``Full'' model uses all three sources and attains 89\% overall accuracy for approximately 58,000 physicians. The ``Name-based'' model uses only NamePrism and WRU, reaching 82\% accuracy for nearly 1 million physicians. The tradeoff is accuracy versus coverage. We use the Full model when all three inputs are available and fall back to the Name-based model otherwise.

\paragraph{Validation.} We validate predictions against verified race information from Florida voter files, Texas medical-license records, and self-reported Zocdoc profiles, matched to our physician sample by name and ZIP code. Table~\ref{tab:accuracy-methods} reports overall accuracy by method. Both random-forest ensembles outperform any individual classifier. Table~\ref{tab:ppv-methods} reports precision (positive predictive value) by race category. The Full model achieves the highest precision for Black and White physicians, while facial recognition alone is more precise for Asian and Hispanic physicians, reflecting the additional signal in profile photos for these groups. Table~\ref{tab:conf-all} presents confusion matrices for the two ensemble models.

The primary measurement concern for our analysis is whether race prediction errors are correlated with the referral covariates (practice affiliation, distance, gender). Because our classifiers rely on names and photos rather than practice or geographic information, prediction errors are unlikely to be systematically related to the organizational and spatial variables that drive our main results. Race concordance is imprecisely estimated in all three specialties, which is consistent with some degree of measurement error in the race variable.

\section{Link-level Conditional Logit}
\label{sec:linkcl}

Table~\ref{tab:linkcl-all} presents marginal effects from a logistic regression for each specialty, analogous to Table 5 in \cite{zeltzer2020}. The dependent variable is a link dummy, set to one if a referral link is present and 0 otherwise, and covariates include demographic concordance, practice and geographic proximity, and year and PCP fixed effects. Following \cite{zeltzer2020}, this analysis does not include specialist fixed effects.

Many of the estimates are broadly consistent with the results from \cite{zeltzer2020}. For example, in both sets of results, organizational proximity dominates. Sharing a practice increases the probability of a link by 7--11 percentage points across the three specialties. Distance enters with the expected negative sign, matching the quadratic distance term in \cite{zeltzer2020}, and experience, medical-school concordance, and specialist sex have only minor effects in both studies. Gender concordance, however, is economically small and imprecise in our orthopedic data, whereas \cite{zeltzer2020} finds a positive gender-concordance effect of roughly 10\%. One possible explanation is the limited gender variation among orthopedic surgeons (98\% male), though gender concordance is also small in dermatology, where the specialist pool is roughly gender-balanced.

Note that the specification without practice affiliation and distance (column (1) in the orthopedic sample) yields a negative pseudo-$R^2$, indicating that demographic concordance alone fits worse than the null model. This further confirms the dominance of organizational and geographic factors in referral formation.


\section{Robustness: Excluding Practice Affiliation}
\label{sec:noprac}

The large marginal effect of practice affiliation raises the question of whether same-practice referrals reflect physician choice or are partly mechanical consequences of employment structure. To isolate the ``reaching outside the practice'' decision, Table~\ref{tab:noprac} presents the Jochmans two-stage estimates after dropping practice affiliation from the covariates entirely.

Across all three specialties, the remaining coefficients (gender concordance, distance, race, age, experience) are qualitatively similar to the full specification, though magnitudes shift modestly as variation previously absorbed by practice affiliation is redistributed. This suggests that the demographic and geographic determinants of referral formation are not artifacts of collinearity with practice affiliation.


\section{Link Decomposition}
\label{sec:link-decomp}

Our year-by-year dynamics estimate the network formation model separately on the referrals made in each post-move year. A natural question is whether the resulting change in the determinants reflects a genuine shift in behavior or a change in the composition of the links formed across years. To address this, Table~\ref{tab:decomp} decomposes the link stock at each post-move year into persisting ties, present in the previous year, and newly formed ties.

If the change were compositional, newly formed links in later years would show concordance rates similar to persisting links. Instead, across all three specialties, newly formed links exhibit progressively lower same-practice rates and modestly lower demographic concordance than persisting links. This pattern is consistent with a genuine shift in how PCPs form new links rather than a mechanical change in link composition.


\section{Dynamics by Specialty}
\label{sec:period-specific}

The main text overlays the year-by-year marginal effects across the three specialties. Figure~\ref{fig:period} presents the same estimates one specialty at a time, showing every covariate for each specialty. The model is estimated separately on the referrals made in each individual post-move year, so each year's choice set is independent.

The per-specialty view makes the heterogeneity in the practice-affiliation dynamics explicit. In orthopedic surgery the effect of practice affiliation is largest in the first post-move year and attenuates thereafter, whereas in cardiology and dermatology it does not decline. The per-year estimates are noisier than the pooled first-year estimate because each year's sample is smaller.


\section{Measuring Specialist Quality}
\label{sec:welfare}

\paragraph{Measuring specialist quality.} We measure the quality of each specialist from the risk-adjusted outcomes of the Medicare patients they treat. For every specialist in our analysis, we identify the patients they saw between 2010 and 2016 in the 100\% Medicare fee-for-service claims and follow each patient for two years after the encounter. Over that window we construct three outcomes, namely total spending across inpatient, outpatient, and carrier claims, non-elective admissions (those not coded as elective), and two-year mortality. Total spending and non-elective admissions serve as our primary measures, since they are the most reliably estimated. We report two-year mortality as a check that reallocation does no harm to the most severe outcome.

\paragraph{Risk adjustment.} Because sicker patients sort to specialists in ways unrelated to specialist performance, we adjust each outcome for patient case mix. We regress each outcome on the Charlson comorbidity index, constructed from the diagnosis codes on each patient's claims prior to the encounter, together with age, sex, race, and dual-eligibility status, and we retain the residual. This residual is the portion of a patient's outcome not explained by their observed health and demographics. We estimate the adjustment separately within each specialty, so that a specialist's quality is measured relative to other physicians treating comparable patients in the same specialty.

\paragraph{Specialist-level measure.} We average each patient's risk-adjusted outcome to the treating specialist, giving a mean residual per specialist on each of the three outcomes. A specialist whose patients have systematically lower spending or fewer admissions than their case mix would predict receives a lower value, which we read as better performance. We compute the measure only for specialists with at least eleven patients, a threshold that retains 99.7\% of patients while dropping the smallest-volume specialists. Because averages over few patients are noisy, we apply empirical-Bayes shrinkage within each specialty, pulling each specialist's mean toward the specialty average in proportion to its imprecision, so that low-volume specialists are not spuriously placed at the extremes. The resulting measure is descriptive. It summarizes the outcomes experienced by a specialist's patients net of observed case mix, and we do not interpret it as the causal effect of seeing a particular specialist.
