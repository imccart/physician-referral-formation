\begin{abstract}
\noindent A referral often determines which expert a client sees, shaping the cost and outcome of the service they receive, yet the referrer may favor organizational convenience over the quality of the match. We study this in referrals from primary care physicians (PCPs) to specialists, focusing on those who move to a new market and build their networks from scratch. Using the universe of 2010--2018 Medicare fee-for-service claims for orthopedic surgery, cardiology, and dermatology, we estimate a network-formation model with two-sided fixed effects. Holding specialist quality fixed, we find that referral networks are shaped most strongly by practice affiliation, followed by geographic proximity, with demographic homophily playing only a minor role. This affiliation effect fades over the post-move years in orthopedic surgery but persists in cardiology and dermatology. There, convenience directs patients toward specialists with higher risk-adjusted spending and more non-elective admissions, a cost that endures well beyond the year of formation.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Expert referrals shape the allocation of high-stakes services in many industries, including finance, law, and health care. Ideally, these referrals align with the quality of the expert they choose. In practice, however, referring intermediaries operate within particular organizational and relational structures, and they may favor experts who are affiliated, familiar, or easy to coordinate with \citep{shumsky2003, whaley2024-jpube, lin2025}, even when a more distant expert would be a better match \citep{devaan2024}. One reason is that a firm can coordinate, monitor, and reward work more easily within its own boundaries than across them \citep{holmstrom1998}. Nonetheless, when convenience prevails over match quality, clients may be routed to experts who serve them less well, and the extent and costs of that misallocation are not well understood. 

We examine the magnitude and implications of convenience-driven referral networks in the context of referrals from primary care physicians (PCPs) to specialists, a setting that is particularly salient given the role these referrals play in directing patient care. When a PCP refers a patient to a specialist, that decision is made under potentially severe information frictions, and yet the referral matters significantly as it fixes the locus of subsequent treatment. The U.S. health care system is increasingly reliant on such referrals, with as many as 30\% of visits to PCPs resulting in a referral to a specialist for additional care \citep{wright2022}, a near two-fold increase in referral rates over the preceding decade \citep{barnett2012aim, mehrotra2011}, and a heavy reliance on physician recommendations among patients \citep{chernew2021}. These initial ties are also durable. Between one fifth and one quarter of the referrals a PCP makes in the four years after forming their network go to specialists they were already referring to in the first year, five to ten times the share we would expect if later referrals were unrelated to the initial network.\footnote{We compute this among PCPs whose networks we observe from formation, as the share of their referrals over the following four years that go to specialists already in the first-year network. Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} reports a related but distinct measure, the per-specialist probability that a first-year tie is referred to again in a later year, which uses a different denominator and is not directly comparable to this share.} The network a PCP forms at the outset therefore continues to shape where patients are sent well beyond the initial period. Yet the influence of these forces is hard to separate from accumulated history, since in an established network a referral tie may reflect the convenience of the pairing or the experience built up over years of referrals, and the two are difficult to tell apart.

To measure the role of convenience without confounding from accumulated history, we exploit PCPs who move into a new, geographically distinct market for which they have no prior referral experience.\footnote{This mover strategy requires that PCPs do not relocate specifically because of the market's set of available specialist referrals, which is a plausible assumption in our setting and supported in our data (see Tables~\ref{tab:desc-doctors} and \ref{tab:desc-specialists}).} Using 100\% Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) claims from 2010--2018, we study referral formation across three specialty areas that span a range of clinical settings and physician demographics, namely orthopedic surgery, cardiology, and dermatology. For each specialty, we estimate a network-formation model with two-sided fixed effects for both the referring PCP and the specialist. Standard conditional logit estimation of such models is inconsistent due to the incidental parameters problem, so we adopt the quadruple-based estimator of \cite{jochmans2018}, which differences out both sets of fixed effects. We then recover the fixed effects in a second stage in order to compute marginal effects on the probability scale. A simulation exercise, presented in the supplemental appendix, confirms that this two-stage procedure can recover the true coefficient estimates and marginal effects, offering a substantial improvement to the analogous one-sided fixed effect estimates and marginal effects via standard conditional logit. Because the two-sided fixed effects absorb each specialist's overall quality and desirability along with each PCP's propensity to refer, the covariates isolate what we term convenience, namely the organizational affiliation, geographic proximity, and demographic similarity that shape a referral once specialist quality is held fixed.

% Results: Initial formation
Across all three specialties, practice affiliation is the dominant predictor of initial referral formation. The effect is largest in orthopedic surgery, where sharing a practice group raises the referral probability by roughly 45 percentage points (95\% CI 33 to 56), followed by dermatology (41 percentage points) and cardiology (25 percentage points). Greater distance consistently lowers the referral probability by 4 to 7 percentage points per five miles. Gender concordance is meaningful in orthopedic surgery (7 percentage points) and small but positive in dermatology (2 percentage points), while negligible for cardiology. Race, age, and experience concordance have economically small effects across all specialties.

% Results: Identifying the practice-affiliation effect
Because practice affiliation is the dominant effect, and a mover who chose a practice for its specialists would refer within it regardless of any organizational channel, we consider two additional designs, neither of which relies on the mover's choice of practice. The first exploits specialists' own moves between practices, and the second uses cross-specialty variation in the composition of a mover's own practice. Both isolate variation in practice affiliation that is plausibly orthogonal to a PCP's practice selection, and both continue to find a significant and meaningful effect of shared practice on referral formation.

% Results: Dynamics
We also study how these referral networks evolve as PCPs spend more time in their new market. Estimating our network formation model separately on the referrals made in each post-move year, we find that the effect of practice affiliation attenuates over the four post-move years in orthopedic surgery, falling from roughly 45 percentage points at entry to about 20 by the fourth year, while it holds steady or rises in cardiology and dermatology. Orthopedic surgery is the setting in which a specialist's contribution is most visible to the referring PCP, a discrete and planned surgical episode, and the attenuation there is consistent with PCPs relying less on organizational convenience as they learn about local specialists.\footnote{The pattern is robust to decomposing links into those persisting from the first post-move year and those newly formed, indicating genuine behavioral change rather than a shifting composition of referrals.} Gender concordance also dissipates over time in orthopedic surgery, while geographic proximity remains a persistent factor across all three specialties. Decomposing the practice affiliation effect, most of it persists after accounting for the referral patterns of a PCP's new practice peers, with a modest part reflecting PCPs mirroring those peers, consistent with limited information transmission within practices. The dynamics therefore point to organizational convenience giving way where specialist quality is most observable, and persisting where it is not.

% Results: Welfare
We then examine what these referral preferences imply for the cost and outcomes of care. For each specialist we summarize the risk-adjusted two-year outcomes of their Medicare patients, namely spending, non-elective admissions, and mortality, and we compute how much each referral determinant shifts referrals toward higher- or lower-utilization specialists by setting its coefficient to zero and recomputing the model-implied referral probabilities. In cardiology and dermatology, organizational affiliation and geographic proximity are associated with referrals to higher-spending, higher-admission specialists, while in orthopedic surgery these effects are smaller and mixed in sign. The effects load on utilization rather than survival, as the associated differences in mortality are small and generally include zero. Demographic concordance has economically negligible consequences for these outcomes in all three specialties. These estimates measure the risk-adjusted outcomes of the specialists to whom convenience directs referrals, not the causal effect on any one patient of being sent to a different specialist.

% Contribution 1: Referral networks
Our analysis contributes to three areas. First, we contribute to the literature on physician referral networks. Much of this work studies networks through shared patients \citep{landon2012, barnett2012mc, landon2018, linde2019}, treating a link as an undirected relationship in which patients flow between two physicians, while a smaller literature studies the directed networks that connect a referring physician to a specialist \citep{agha2019, agha2018, zeltzer2020}. These networks are typically taken as given and studied as an input into care. By observing referral networks at formation, we can instead identify the effects of characteristics such as organizational convenience, without the confounding from accumulated history that arises once a network is established. We are closest to \cite{zeltzer2020}, who documents gender homophily in specialist referrals, but we cover three specialty areas separately rather than pooling across conditions, and we use a mover design with a two-sided fixed-effects estimator that accommodates unobserved PCP and specialist heterogeneity \citep{jochmans2018, jochmans_fixedeffect_2019, graham_econometric_2017, dzemski2019}. The finding itself echoes \cite{devaan2024}, who show that strong referral ties reduce referrals to better-matched but more distant specialists, though in a single state and within established relationships. Our mover design identifies the same pattern as networks form across the national Medicare population, free of accumulated history. Our focus on formation also distinguishes us from work on how referral relationships evolve through learning \citep{mccarthy2026}, where the question is how a network improves with experience rather than how it first takes shape.

% Contribution 2: Organizational affiliation and referrals
Second, we contribute to the literature on how organizational affiliation shapes physician referrals. A large body of work studies a specific and policy-salient form of affiliation, the ownership of physician practices by hospitals and larger firms, and how integration redirects a physician's referrals \citep{baker2016, walden2016, singh2023, whaley2024-jpube, cutler2026}. This connects to the broader literature on physician agency, since the referral is where a PCP's decision-making authority is most consequential for a patient \citep{freedman2015, barkowski2018, chernew2021, lin2025}. Our analysis speaks to the same question but takes a broader view of affiliation, defined by whether a PCP and specialist share a practice rather than by a particular form of ownership or integration. Because our movers arrive with no prior referral ties, we can measure its effect separately from the relationships a physician has already built.

% Contribution 3: The cost of convenience-driven referrals
Third, we connect these referral patterns to the cost and outcomes of care. Much of the geographic variation in health spending reflects provider behavior rather than patient need, with physician practice style the dominant factor \citep{finkelstein2016, molitor2018, badinski2023}, and referral is one such behavior. Attaching risk-adjusted outcomes to each specialist, we find that in cardiology and dermatology convenience directs patients toward specialists with higher spending and more non-elective admissions, but no higher mortality. Importantly, these costs are not confined to the year of formation, since networks formed in part through convenience persist for many years afterward.


\section{PCP Referral Networks}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{A Model of Network Formation}
Our analysis follows a conceptual model in which a PCP's decision to initiate a referral relationship with a specialist is a function of three broad factors: 1) similarity in observable characteristics between the PCP and the specialist, denoted by $x_{ij}$; 2) the utility value of unobserved characteristics, denoted $\alpha_{ij}$; and 3) idiosyncratic shocks that affect the formation of a referral link, denoted by $\varepsilon_{ij}$. Note that this setup considers the network formation decision of a PCP, not a given referral, and the outcome of interest is ultimately whether a PCP has any referral relationship with a given specialist. With this setup, a referral link between PCP $i$ and specialist $j$ will exist if the latent utility of such a relationship is positive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:referral}
    y_{ij} = \mathbf{1} \left(x_{ij}'\beta + \alpha_{ij} - \varepsilon_{ij} \geq 0\right),
\end{equation}
where $y_{ij}$ takes the value of 1 if PCP $i$ refers to specialist $j$ and 0 otherwise. In our application, the vector of observed characteristics, $x_{ij}$, captures several measures of practice and demographic homophily, including working in the same practice group, differential years of experience, and homophily in gender and race. Geographic proximity also enters the model through $x_{ij}$, which we measure as the distance between PCP and specialist practice locations.

We parameterize the unobserved component as $\alpha_{ij} = \alpha_{i} + \alpha_{j}$, where $\alpha_{i}$ and $\alpha_{j}$ are individual-specific effects for PCPs and specialists, respectively. The PCP effect $\alpha_{i}$ reflects, for example, a PCP's productivity or propensity to refer, and $\alpha_{j}$ similarly captures specialists' time-invariant prestige or quality (over the relative short run). In principle, physicians who practice in the same geographic area may also share unobserved preferences that make a referral link more likely, independent of their observable characteristics. We address this sorting concern through our mover-based research design rather than through additional model parameters, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:identification}.

Lastly, the error term, $\varepsilon_{ij}$, captures idiosyncratic shocks that affect the formation of a referral link. Under the assumption that $\varepsilon_{ij}$ follows a Type I extreme value distribution, the probability of observing a referral relationship from PCP $i$ to specialist $j$ takes the logistic form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:probability}
    \operatorname{Pr}(y_{ij} = 1 | x_{ij}) = \frac{e^{x_{ij}'\beta + \alpha_{ij}}}{1 + e^{x_{ij}'\beta + \alpha_{ij}}}.
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:referral} and \eqref{eq:probability} therefore capture a reduced-form analog to a network formation model that accounts for unobserved heterogeneity of doctors and specialists. 

Link formation based on observed characteristics, $x_{ij}$, and unobserved heterogeneity, $\alpha_{ij}$, describe two distinct features of real world networks, namely sorting on observables (including homophily, distance, etc.) and degree heterogeneity. These modeling choices mirror a large empirical literature on homophily and degree heterogeneity in physician networks. Homophilous matching on practice environment and personal traits has been documented for patient-sharing ties \citep{barnett2012mc,landon_using_2013} and for gender-concordant referrals \citep{zeltzer2020,sarsons2023}. Degree dispersion, captured here by the PCP- and specialist-specific effects $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_j$, also follows that of recent network-formation models such as \cite{jackson2007}, \cite{graham_econometric_2017}, and \cite{dzemski2019}. The key parameter of interest in Equation \ref{eq:probability} is $\beta$, which captures the effect of observed characteristics on the referral formation.


\subsection{Identification}
\label{sec:identification}

Our identification strategy rests on two design choices. The first is a focus on PCP ``movers,'' physicians who relocate to a non-contiguous Hospital Referral Region (HRR) and therefore arrive in a new market with no previously observed local referral ties. Established PCP-specialist links embed years of accumulated learning and habit formation, so an analysis that pools all referrals may conflate initial link formation with the subsequent evolution of relationships, ultimately introducing potential bias in our estimate of $\beta$. Focusing on physicians at the moment they enter a new HRR lets us observe referral choices made before such path dependence arises and better isolates the forces that govern where and how initial links form. 

Under the assumption that PCPs do not relocate to a specific HRR in order to refer to particular specialists, this design ultimately helps to eliminate correlation between unobserved PCP and specialist characteristics that arises from shared location preferences. This assumption is plausible if, for example, physician relocations are driven by broader career opportunities, family considerations, or institutional recruitment rather than by the composition of the local specialist pool. A violation would require PCPs to select their destination HRR based on the demographic or practice characteristics of specialists in that market. If such selection were present, it would likely bias our estimates upward, as PCPs who sort into markets with demographically similar specialists would exhibit higher concordance than PCPs who relocate for unrelated reasons. To assess the reasonableness of this assumption, Tables~\ref{tab:desc-doctors} and \ref{tab:desc-specialists} show that movers are broadly similar to the full PCP and specialist samples on race and geographic proximity, with modest differences in age and gender consistent with relocation being more common earlier in a physician's career. This pattern supports the view that moves are not driven by the composition of the destination specialist pool. More broadly, a mover enters the destination HRR with no previously observed referral ties to any specialist in that market, so any correlation between a mover's referral choices and a specialist's characteristics must arise after arrival rather than reflect relationships carried over from the origin. This feature of the design addresses a broad class of endogeneity concerns at once, rather than for any single covariate. As we detail in the supplemental appendix, movers are also not a distinctive group of PCPs apart from their mobility. They are somewhat younger and less likely to be male, and they tend to relocate to larger and more specialist-dense markets, as relocating physicians generally do, but before their move they are no more active or unusual as referrers than comparable non-movers.

A further concern is that movers select the markets or practices they join based on the referral opportunities they offer, a form of selection that the mover design does not by itself rule out. We examine the market dimension in the supplemental appendix and find that movers' destinations do not differ from their origins in ways that would bias our estimates, and we address the practice dimension directly in Section~\ref{sec:practice-id}, where two designs hold the mover's choice of practice fixed.

The second design choice is the use of discordant quadruples, following \cite{jochmans2018}. Each quadruple $m$ consists of two PCPs $(i, k)$ and two specialists $(j, l)$. We transform the outcome $y_{ij}$ and characteristics $x_{ij}$ as
\begin{align*}
    \tilde{y}_m & = \frac{(y_{ij} - y_{il}) - (y_{kj} - y_{kl})}{2} \text{ and} \\
    \tilde{x}_m & = (x_{ij} - x_{il}) - (x_{kj} - x_{kl}).
\end{align*}
This double-differencing is conceptually analogous to a difference-in-differences estimator. The within-PCP differences, $(y_{ij} - y_{il})$ and $(y_{kj} - y_{kl})$, each remove their respective PCP fixed effect, leaving only the specialist difference $\alpha_j - \alpha_l$. Differencing across the two PCPs then eliminates the specialist fixed effects as well, so that identification of $\beta$ derives entirely from $\tilde{y} \in \{-1, 1\}$ (i.e., from quadruples in which the two PCPs rank the two specialists differently). Quadruples in which the two PCPs agree contribute no information and are discarded. This is an extension of the conditional logit model from \cite{chamberlain_analysis_1980} to the two-sided fixed effects setting. Alternative estimators such as \cite{graham_econometric_2017} and \cite{dzemski2019} jointly estimate the fixed effects and homophily parameters and apply bias corrections in the estimates of the homophily parameters. We instead adopt the quadruple-based approach because it delivers consistent $\widehat{\beta}$ without requiring a bias correction, and because our setting, unlike the network formation model of \cite{dzemski2019}, involves directed links with no meaningful reciprocity.\footnote{The supplemental appendix presents a link-level conditional logit specification with PCP and year fixed effects, analogous to \citet{zeltzer2020}, which retains the full sample and yields qualitatively similar conclusions. The appendix also compares physicians included in the Jochmans discordant quadruples to those excluded, finding broadly similar observable characteristics across the two groups.} 


\subsection{Estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

Following Section \ref{sec:identification}, we estimate $\beta$ by maximizing the log-likelihood function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:likelihood}
    L(\beta) = \sum_{m=1}^M 1\{\tilde{y}_m=-1\} \times \log \Lambda(\tilde{x}_m'\beta) \ + \ 1\{\tilde{y}_m=1\} \times \log (1-\Lambda(\tilde{x}_m'\beta)),
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda(\cdot)$ is the logistic CDF and the summation runs over all discordant quadruples. We cluster standard errors at the HRR level to account for within-market dependence across quadruples.

While the Jochmans estimator delivers consistent estimates of $\beta$, it also eliminates the fixed effects $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_j$ and therefore cannot produce predicted referral probabilities directly. To recover the full index $x_{ij}'\beta + \alpha_i + \alpha_j$ needed for marginal effects and our analysis of referral outcomes, we employ a two-stage procedure. In the first stage, we estimate $\hat{\beta}$ from the quadruple-based conditional logit estimated from Equation \eqref{eq:likelihood}. In the second stage, we hold $\hat{\beta}$ fixed, enter the linear index $x_{ij}'\hat{\beta}$ as an offset, and recover the fixed effects from the model $\operatorname{Pr}(y_{ij}=1) = \Lambda(\alpha_i + \alpha_j + x_{ij}'\hat{\beta})$, where $x_{ij}'\hat{\beta}$ is treated as known. We recover the two sets of fixed effects in sequence. Because movers make only a handful of referrals each, most specialists are never referred to by any mover, and their effects $\alpha_j$ cannot be recovered from the mover sample alone. We therefore first estimate the specialist effects on the full sample of referring PCPs, movers and non-movers alike, where a specialist referred to at least once is identified, winsorizing at $\pm 6$ log-odds the small number of effects that remain perfectly separated. Holding these specialist effects fixed, we then recover the PCP effects on the mover sample. The specialist effect is common to all referrers under the model, so estimating it from the full referring population adds data rather than assumptions. The recovered effects, combined with $\hat{\beta}$, yield predicted referral probabilities for each PCP-specialist pair. Average marginal effects are computed as discrete changes in the predicted probabilities, a unit change for binary covariates and a 5-mile change for distance. We average these changes across pairs with weights equal to the predicted referral probability, so that the effect reflects pairs where a referral is otherwise plausible rather than being diluted over the many choice-set pairs for which a referral is highly unlikely. Standard errors are computed by the delta method. We validate this two-stage procedure via Monte Carlo simulation in the supplemental appendix, showing that our approach recovers true parameters under varying degrees of FE-covariate correlation.\footnote{The conditional logit with one-sided fixed effects does not require the quadruple construction and can be estimated on the full choice set. Marginal effects from this specification are generally attenuated relative to the two-stage estimates, consistent with omitted specialist heterogeneity biasing the estimates toward zero. Nonetheless, the supplemental appendix also presents standard conditional logit results conditioning on PCP fixed effects only.} 

Estimating the specialist effects on the full referring population, rather than on movers alone, is what makes the second stage feasible. To identify a specialist effect $\alpha_j$ we need at least one PCP who refers to $j$, and with movers making only a handful of referrals each, many specialists appear in movers' choice sets but are never referred to by any mover. Non-movers refer to these same specialists in far greater numbers, so estimating $\alpha_j$ on the full sample identifies all but a small residual set, which we winsorize. We confirm in two ways that the full-sample specialist effects carry over to movers' referrals, with full details in the supplemental appendix. First, we enter the estimated specialist effects into the mover-referral model as a single regressor with coefficient $\lambda$, where $\lambda = 0$ would indicate no predictive value for movers and $\lambda = 1$ that the effects transfer at full strength. We reject $\lambda = 0$ in every specialty, and we cannot reject $\lambda = 1$ in orthopedic surgery and dermatology, with $\lambda$ only modestly below one in cardiology. Second, the recovered marginal effects are stable across a wide range of iteration counts (50 to 250), indicating that they are not sensitive to the convergence criterion.

\subsection{Dynamics}
\label{sec:dynamics-method}

Our mover design also provides a natural framework for studying dynamic effects of referral network formation, separately from the initial network. Rather than observing only the first post-move year, we estimate the network formation model separately on the referrals made in each post-move year, so the first year captures the initial network and each later year captures the referrals formed in that year alone. Comparing the estimated marginal effects across these years reveals whether the factors driving referral formation persist, strengthen, or attenuate as PCPs accumulate local experience.

Because each year is estimated on its own referrals, the comparison across years is not subject to the mechanical dilution that would arise from a widening cumulative window. As a further check on whether any change reflects genuine behavior rather than a shifting composition of referrals, we decompose links into persisting ties (present since the first year) and newly formed ties, and examine whether the new links differ systematically from the persisting ones.

Our dynamics analysis characterizes how the determinants of referral formation change over time but does not identify the specific mechanisms driving that change. For example, PCPs may update their referral networks as they learn about specialist quality from patient outcomes. Distinguishing among such mechanisms is beyond the scope of this paper but is the subject of ongoing work \citep{mccarthy2026}.

\section{Data Sources and Sample Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Our analysis relies on the 100\% Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) claims files from 2008--2018 (2008 and 2009 serve only as a lookback window), in particular the Medicare Part A (inpatient) and Part B (carrier) files. Data on physician characteristics come from CMS Physician Compare, the Medicare Data on Provider Practice and Specialty (MD-PPAS), and the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System (NPPES). Demographic data from Physician Compare are first available in 2012; however, we rely on those data for time-invariant physician characteristics such as year of medical school graduation.\footnote{CMS Physician Compare also includes data on which medical school a physician attended; however, these data are sparse. Because our quadruple-based estimator requires each variable to be observed for all four physicians in a quadruple, even moderate missingness in the raw data translates to substantial sample loss in the estimation sample, and we therefore exclude medical school from our analysis.} As such, our data construction is relatively unaffected by the late arrival of Physician Compare data.

Across all three specialties, we infer the referring PCP as the primary care physician with the highest number of office-based evaluation and management (E\&M) visits in the twelve months before the specialist encounter, following \cite{pham2009} and \cite{agha2019}, provided the patient saw that PCP at least twice.\footnote{An alternative approach uses the referring physician NPI (\texttt{rfr\_physn\_npi}) recorded on the specialist's claim, but this can be an unreliable measure of the PCP. For example, in the case of orthopedic surgery, this field on the inpatient claim is not populated in roughly 32\% of cases, and when populated, it most often lists the surgeon themselves (about 43\%) or a non-PCP specialist (about 7\%), with only 18\% listing a PCP. Our carrier-lookback method identifies a PCP for approximately 80\% of orthopedic procedures and, among cases where both methods identify a PCP, concordance exceeds 82\% in every year.} The lookback period for PCP identification along with our mover design effectively consume up to two years of data. Our analysis sample therefore spans 2010--2018. We further limit the sample to PCPs and specialists each with at least 10 encounters over the full 2010--2018 period.

We study referral formation across three specialty areas, namely orthopedic surgery, cardiology, and dermatology. We selected these different specialties so as to consider a range of clinical settings and referral processes. Orthopedic referrals are identified through inpatient admissions for planned and elective major joint replacement via Diagnosis Related Group (DRG) codes, while cardiology and dermatology referrals are identified through new-patient E\&M office visits via Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. The three specialties also differ markedly in their demographic composition and organizational structure. Orthopedic surgeons are nearly all male (98\%), cardiologists are predominantly male but less so than orthopedists (88\%), and dermatologists are roughly gender-balanced (56\% male). In the MD-PPAS data, 3.5\% of practice groups containing a PCP also include a cardiologist, 2.8\% include an orthopedic surgeon, and only 1.2\% include a dermatologist, reflecting the tendency of dermatologists to practice independently or in single-specialty groups.

As discussed in Section \ref{sec:model}, our primary variables of interest consist of physician demographics and practice characteristics. Since our analysis ultimately conditions on both PCP and specialist fixed effects, our key practice characteristic (other than indicators for sharing the same practice) is geographic proximity, measured as the distance between ZIP code centroids of the PCP's primary practice location and that of the specialist. These data are available from MD-PPAS. We construct the differential age as well as years since medical school graduation as a measure of similarity in medical experience. These data come from CMS Physician Compare. We also construct measures of gender homophily, again from data in MD-PPAS.

Because physician race is not publicly available on a national scale, we impute physicians' race by combining three complementary classifiers. First, we obtain name-based probabilities from NamePrism and from the WRU Bayesian surname-geocoding algorithm. Second, we obtain profile photos from publicly available Zocdoc physician profile pages and use the DeepFace network to generate image-based probabilities. After aligning category labels across methods, we aggregate the separate predictions with a random-forest ensemble. A ``Full'' model (using all three sources) attains 89\% accuracy for approximately 58,000 physicians, while a ``Name-based'' model (names only) reaches 82\% accuracy for nearly 1 million physicians. Accuracy is assessed against verified race information drawn from Florida voter files, Texas medical-license records, and self-reported Zocdoc profiles. Full implementation details and accuracy are discussed in the supplemental appendix.

We flag a PCP as a mover in the first year they practice in a new HRR. For every calendar year, we build a two-year window $\{t-1,t\}$, extract each doctor's HRR in the MD-PPAS panel, and keep those whose HRR in year $t-1$ differs from the HRR in year $t$. To exclude cosmetic boundary shifts, we remove cases in which the origin and destination HRRs are geographic neighbors, identified from the Dartmouth Atlas HRR shapefile and its contiguity matrix. We further drop PCPs who register more than one HRR change inside the window, so every retained observation reflects a single, non-contiguous relocation. A PCP who relocates more than once across the panel (e.g., in 2012 and again in 2016) may appear in multiple annual cohorts, but the quadruples used for estimation are formed within a single HRR-year cell, so repeat movers do not introduce mechanical dependence across cohorts. Figure \ref{fig:movers} maps the origin-destination flows for PCP movers, with each arc connecting a mover's pre- and post-relocation HRR, limited to highest frequency origin and destination HRRs for brevity. The flows span the full geography of the United States, with no dominant corridor or regional concentration. This geographic dispersion is important for our identification strategy, as it suggests that the mover sample is not driven by a small number of local labor market shocks. 

For our network formation model and subsequent estimation, a PCP is assumed to construct their referral network as a subset of all possible specialists in their HRR. Referral counts among any given PCP-specialist pair are on average small. As such, we define PCP-specialist links as any observed referral between a given PCP and specialist, effectively ignoring the intensive margin.

\subsection{Specialty-Specific Sample Construction}

\paragraph{Orthopedic surgery.} Major joint replacement is among the most common inpatient surgical procedures for Medicare beneficiaries. Since we limit to planned and elective inpatient procedures, the PCP's referral to an orthopedic surgeon typically occurs well in advance of the surgery, making the referral decision relatively deliberate.\footnote{While joint replacements can now take place in an outpatient setting, hip and knee replacements remained on the inpatient-only list over essentially the entirety of our data. Total knee replacements, for example, were only reimbursable in an outpatient setting as of January 2018, and hip replacements two years later in 2020.} Orthopedic surgery is also one of the most male-dominated specialties, with 98\% of surgeons in our data being male. Among practice groups that contain at least one PCP, 2.8\% also include an orthopedic surgeon. We identify eligible procedures by DRG codes 461, 462, 469, 470, 480--489, and 507--511, keeping only cases performed by an orthopedic surgeon with an identifiable referring PCP. Our orthopedic sample consists of approximately 566,000 PCP-specialist pairs, roughly 64,000 unique PCPs, around 15,200 unique specialists, and 1,234 PCP movers.

\paragraph{Cardiology.} Cardiology referrals in our data are based on new-patient E\&M office visits (CPT codes 99201--99205) performed by cardiologists and clinical cardiac electrophysiologists. Unlike orthopedic surgery, these are initial consultations for evaluation rather than discrete surgical procedures, ranging from routine assessments (e.g., lipid management, hypertension follow-up) to more urgent evaluations (e.g., chest pain, arrhythmia workup). We focus on E\&M visits rather than cardiac procedures because the PCP-to-surgeon referral chain for cardiology procedures is less direct, with PCPs often referring to a general cardiologist who may then refer onward for procedural care. Cardiology is predominantly male (88\% of specialists), though less so than orthopedic surgery. Among PCP-containing practice groups, 3.5\% also include a cardiologist, the highest share among the three specialties, reflecting the integration of cardiology into hospital-based and multispecialty group practices. Our cardiology sample consists of 1.67 million PCP-specialist pairs, roughly 113,000 unique PCPs, around 23,000 unique specialists, and 5,071 PCP movers.

\paragraph{Dermatology.} Dermatology referrals are based on the same new-patient E\&M CPT codes used for cardiology. The majority of dermatology referrals are for relatively lower-acuity conditions such as skin checks, rashes, and acne, though skin cancer screening and evaluation can carry higher clinical stakes. Dermatology is also the most gender-balanced specialty in our analysis, with 56\% male specialists, and only 1.2\% of PCP-containing practice groups include a dermatologist, the lowest share among the three specialties. This organizational separation makes dermatology a useful contrast to cardiology, where multispecialty group integration is more common. Our dermatology sample consists of 1.60 million PCP-specialist pairs, roughly 116,000 unique PCPs, around 13,000 unique specialists, and 4,664 PCP movers.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}
\label{sec:desc}

Tables~\ref{tab:desc-doctors} and \ref{tab:desc-specialists} summarize physician and referral characteristics for each specialty. Across all three, PCP movers are younger and less male than the full PCP population, consistent with younger physicians being more geographically mobile. The typical PCP refers to 2--3 distinct specialists, with movers initially referring to fewer specialists immediately after relocating. Specialist characteristics vary with the specialty's demographic composition. Orthopedic surgeons are nearly all male (98\%), cardiologists are predominantly male (88\%), and dermatologists are roughly gender-balanced (56\% male). Dermatologists have the highest median in-degree (27 referring PCPs), consistent with fewer dermatologists serving larger catchment areas.

Table~\ref{tab:link-stats} contrasts observable concordance between established and non-established referral links across all three specialties. Organizational proximity and geography dominate the contrast in every specialty. Same-practice rates among established links range from 12.2\% in dermatology to 24.8\% in cardiology, compared to 2--3\% among non-links. Average distance is roughly 10--11 miles for established links versus 29--42 miles for non-links. Demographic concordance differs more modestly between established and non-established links, with gender and race concordance gaps of 1--5 percentage points.

\section{Initial Network Formation}
\label{sec:formation}

Because these estimates come from the moment a PCP forms their network, they isolate the role of practice affiliation, proximity, and similarity before prior experience with local specialists can shape a referral. Table~\ref{tab:logit-twfe} presents the estimation results for all three specialties. Columns (1)--(3) report odds ratios from the full-specification Jochmans estimator, while columns (4)--(6) present the corresponding average marginal effects from our two-stage procedure. Progressive specifications are available in the supplemental appendix. Figure~\ref{fig:mfx-cross} summarizes our results in a forest plot of the full-specification marginal effects across all three specialties. We discuss specific results below, focusing on average marginal effects for practice affiliation, geographic proximity, and demographic concordance. 

\paragraph{Practice affiliation.} Sharing a practice group is the dominant predictor of referral formation in all three specialties, but the magnitude varies substantially. The marginal effect is largest in orthopedic surgery (45 percentage points), followed by dermatology (41) and cardiology (25). This ordering does not simply mirror same-practice link rates. Cardiology has the highest share of same-practice links (24.8\%) yet the smallest marginal effect, while orthopedic surgery has an intermediate same-practice link rate (18.9\%) and the largest effect. How organizational ties translate into referral decisions appears to depend on institutional features that differ across specialties, such as the nature of the referral (surgical vs.\ consultative), the degree of integration into multispecialty groups, and the clinical urgency of the referral. Because practice affiliation is so dominant, the supplemental appendix presents a robustness specification that drops it entirely, isolating the ``reaching outside the practice'' decision. In that specification, distance and gender concordance retain their signs and significance across all three specialties, confirming that these factors operate independently of practice affiliation. The supplemental appendix further shows that movers do not relocate to markets more oriented toward within-practice referral, and that movers are not unusually within-practice-oriented referrers, mitigating the concern that this estimate reflects selection into referral-facilitating environments.

\paragraph{Geographic proximity.} Distance is a robust and persistent predictor across all three specialties, with marginal effects ranging from 4 to 7 percentage points per five miles. The penalty is comparable across specialties, somewhat larger in dermatology and orthopedic surgery than in cardiology.

\paragraph{Demographic concordance.} Gender concordance is largest in orthopedic surgery (7 percentage points), small in dermatology (2 percentage points), and negligible in cardiology. This pattern does not scale with the degree of gender variation in the specialist pool. Orthopedic surgery, where specialists are nearly all male, shows the largest gender effect, while dermatology, with a roughly gender-balanced specialist pool, shows a much smaller effect. Race concordance is imprecisely estimated in all three specialties. Age and experience play no meaningful role anywhere.

\section{Dynamics in Network Formation}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The reliance on convenience we see at formation may fade as a PCP gains experience with the local specialist pool, or it may endure. Table~\ref{tab:dynamics} documents how PCP referral networks evolve as PCPs spend one to four years in their new market. Across all three specialties, networks expand substantially, from 1.7 to 3.9 distinct specialists in orthopedic surgery, 2.4 to 6.2 in cardiology, and 2.3 to 5.9 in dermatology. The faster expansion in cardiology and dermatology reflects the higher frequency of E\&M consultations relative to inpatient surgical procedures. In all three specialties, the share of within-practice referral links declines as networks mature, and mean distance increases, suggesting that PCPs gradually reach beyond their immediate organizational environment.

Figure~\ref{fig:mfx-window-cross} presents the estimated marginal effects by post-move year across all three specialties. The effect of practice affiliation attenuates over the four post-move years in orthopedic surgery, falling from roughly 45 percentage points at entry to about 20 by the fourth year. In cardiology and dermatology it does not, holding near 40 percentage points in dermatology and, if anything, strengthening in cardiology from about 25 to 35 percentage points. Testing these differences formally, the decline in the same-practice marginal effect from the first to the fourth post-move year in orthopedic surgery is statistically significant ($p = 0.004$) and significantly steeper than in cardiology, where the effect does not attenuate (difference $p = 0.002$). The corresponding difference from dermatology, whose effect is roughly flat, is only marginal (difference $p = 0.07$). Orthopedic surgery is the specialty in which a specialist's contribution is most visible to the referring PCP, a discrete and planned surgical episode with well-defined outcomes, while cardiology and dermatology referrals are more consultative and carry a noisier quality signal. That organizational convenience gives way over time where specialist quality is most observable, and persists where it is not, is consistent with PCPs relying less on convenience as they learn about local specialists. Gender concordance dissipates over time in orthopedic surgery, while geographic distance retains a persistent penalty across all specialties.

As discussed in Section \ref{sec:dynamics-method}, a remaining question is whether the orthopedic decline reflects a genuine change in behavior or a shift in the composition of referrals across years. The supplemental appendix decomposes links into persisting ties, present since the first year, and newly formed ties. If the decline were compositional, newly formed ties in later years would resemble the persisting ones. Instead, newly formed ties exhibit progressively lower organizational and demographic concordance, consistent with a genuine shift in how PCPs form new links as they gain local experience.

\subsection{Persistence of the Initial Network}

The preceding results describe how the determinants of new referral formation change as PCPs accumulate experience. A related question is whether the ties a PCP forms on arrival persist. We examine this among movers who remain in their destination HRR after relocating, which describes 80 to 87 percent of movers in our data, and for whom we can follow the initial network over the subsequent post-move years. For each specialist in a mover's first-year choice set, we ask whether the PCP refers to that specialist again in each of the next three years. Specifically, we estimate the linear probability model
$$ y_{ijt} = \delta\,\mathrm{First}_{ij} + x_{ij}'\gamma + \mu_j + \nu_i + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{ijt}, $$
where $y_{ijt}$ equals one if PCP $i$ refers to specialist $j$ in post-move year $t \in \{2,3,4\}$, $\mathrm{First}_{ij}$ equals one if $i$ referred to $j$ in the first post-move year, $x_{ij}$ are the practice and demographic characteristics from our formation model, and $\mu_j$, $\nu_i$, and $\tau_t$ are specialist, PCP, and year fixed effects. The fixed effects absorb a specialist's overall attractiveness and a PCP's referral propensity, so $\delta$ measures how much more likely a first-year tie is to be referred to again than a comparable specialist the PCP did not initially refer to. We report $\delta$ with and without the characteristics $x_{ij}$ that formed the tie, the latter showing whether the persistence is more than those characteristics continuing to predict referral.

Table~\ref{tab:persist} reports the estimates. A specialist in a PCP's first-year network is referred to again in a later year with a probability of roughly one quarter to one third, against about two percent for a comparable specialist the PCP did not initially refer to, or twelve to seventeen times higher. Net of the specialist and PCP fixed effects, a first-year tie carries an 18 to 23 percentage point higher probability of a later referral, and this falls only to 15 to 20 points once we add the characteristics that formed the tie, so the persistence is not merely convenience continuing to operate. The estimates are stable under two-way clustering on the market and the specialist, and a logit specification yields the same conclusion.

The association weakens as PCPs gain local experience, declining from about one quarter in the second post-move year to 11 to 14 percentage points by the fourth. The initial network is thus a durable stock whose pull fades only gradually. In orthopedic surgery this parallels the attenuation of the formation determinants documented above, where the determinants of new links shift away from convenience as PCPs gain local experience even as the ties formed at entry persist. In cardiology and dermatology, where the formation determinants do not attenuate, the persistence of the initial network instead compounds a continued reliance on organizational convenience.

While we cannot definitively separate this durability from a stable underlying match between a PCP and a specialist, it is consistent with a meaningful degree of network persistence. With such persistence, any substitution between specialist quality and organizational, geographic, or demographic convenience in its formation, to the extent one exists, would persist as well.

\section{Identifying the Practice-Affiliation Effect}
\label{sec:practice-id}

Practice affiliation is the dominant determinant of referral formation, and reading it as a
causal channel rather than as sorting rests on more than one piece of evidence. The mover
design in Section~\ref{sec:identification} strips out the accumulated ties and habit that
confound established relationships, and our comparison of movers to the broader physician
population, in Section~\ref{sec:data} and the supplemental appendix, shows that movers are not
a distinctive slice of PCPs. The concern that remains is specific to practice affiliation. A
mover who selects a practice for its roster of specialists would refer within it regardless of
any organizational channel, so the same-practice coefficient conflates practice affiliation with the
sorting of PCPs across practices. We address this concern with two designs, neither of which
relies on the mover's choice of practice. The first exploits the specialists' own moves between
practices, holding the practice fixed and varying a mover's practice affiliation through the specialist's
arrival. The second holds the mover fixed and asks whether the pull toward in-house specialists
follows the specialties in which the mover's new practice happens to run deep, net of proximity,
concordance, and specialist quality.

\subsection{Specialist Moves}
\label{sec:specmoves}

Our first design uses variation that the movers themselves do not choose, namely the
specialists' own moves between practices. Consider a cardiologist who was a member of a group
practice from 2010 to 2015 and then left. A PCP who relocates into that practice in 2013 arrives
while the cardiologist is still there, and is affiliated with that cardiologist. A PCP who relocates
into the same practice in 2016 arrives after the cardiologist has left, and is not affiliated with
that cardiologist. By exploiting the cardiologist's move alongside the timing of the PCP's own
relocation, we identify the effect of practice affiliation free of confounding from the
unobservables that might shape a PCP's original choice of practice.

We
restrict the choice set to specialists who occupied the mover's destination practice at
some point in the sample, and we let $\mathrm{CoPresent}_{ij}$ equal one if specialist $j$ is a
member of that practice in the year mover $i$ arrives, that is, if $i$'s arrival falls within
$j$'s tenure spell there. On this sample we estimate the linear probability model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specmoves}
 y_{ij} = \delta\,\mathrm{CoPresent}_{ij} + x_{ij}'\beta + \alpha_i + \alpha_j + \varepsilon_{ij},
\end{equation}
where $y_{ij}$ equals one if $i$ refers to $j$ in the arrival year, $x_{ij}$ collects distance
and demographic concordance, and $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_j$ are PCP and specialist fixed
effects. Equation~\eqref{eq:specmoves} retains the two-sided fixed-effect structure of our
formation model, and because it is linear the effects enter directly without the
incidental-parameters problem that requires the quadruple estimator in
Section~\ref{sec:estimation}. The parameter $\delta$ is the effect of practice affiliation on the
referral probability. It is identified by comparing pairs in which the specialist was a member of
the mover's destination practice in the arrival year against pairs in which the specialist was a
member at some other point but not that year, having either left before the mover arrived or not
yet joined. We cluster standard errors by Hospital Referral
Region.

The identifying assumption is that a mover's arrival year is independent of when the specialist
moved into or out of the destination practice, conditional on the specialist having been there
at some point. A violation would require movers to time their relocation to a specialist's
presence, which would show up as elevated referral among movers who arrive before the specialist
joins, and we use those before-join movers as a placebo. The variation comes only from mobile
specialists, so $\delta$ is identified from a narrower set of pairs than the practice-affiliation effect in
our main model. The supplemental appendix compares the
mobile specialists to the stayers and finds them broadly similar in demographics and in
risk-adjusted quality, so the specialists supplying this variation are not a sharply selected
group.

Table~\ref{tab:specmoves} reports the results. Net of specialist and PCP fixed effects,
practice affiliation raises the referral probability by 6.5 percentage points in cardiology and 8.0
percentage points in dermatology, both statistically significant. We also estimate a similar but
imprecise effect in orthopedic surgery of 10.7 percentage points. Movers who arrive before the
specialist joins refer no more than baseline, so the effect is not driven by PCP movers timing
their relocation to the specialist's presence. Because this variation comes from the specialist's
move rather than the PCP mover's choice of practice, the effects corroborate the
practice-affiliation effect free of the practice-selection concern.


\subsection{Cross-Specialty Availability}
\label{sec:availability}

The specialist-move design isolates practice affiliation among mobile specialists. Our second
design keeps the full mover sample and draws instead on how the destination practice is composed
across specialties. Movers often refer across more than one specialty, and their practice has more in-house
specialists in some of those specialties than in others. Collapsing the choice set to the mover-by-specialty level, we let
$\pi_{is}$ denote the share of mover $i$'s specialty-$s$ referrals that go to a specialist inside
$i$'s practice, and $a_{is}$ the share of the local specialty-$s$ choice set that sits inside that
practice. We estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:avail}
 \pi_{is} = \lambda\,a_{is} + \alpha_i + u_{is},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_i$ is a PCP fixed effect, so that $\lambda$ is identified within mover from the
difference in in-house availability across their specialties. Under random allocation across the
choice set the in-house share equals in-house availability in expectation, so the relevant null is
$\lambda = 1$ rather than zero, and a slope above one is over-referral to in-house specialists
beyond what their availability alone delivers. We cluster standard errors by Hospital Referral
Region.

A slope above one does not by itself establish practice affiliation, since in-house specialists
tend to be closer and may differ in quality, either of which would raise the in-house share above
availability without any organizational channel. We separate these channels with a benchmark. We
fit a referral model on distance, demographic concordance, and risk-adjusted specialist quality
while excluding practice affiliation, and we aggregate its predictions into a benchmark in-house
share for each mover-specialty. Because the actual share equals this benchmark plus a residual, the
availability slope splits additively into the part the observables deliver and the part
attributable to practice affiliation. The identifying assumption is that, within a mover, the
destination practice's specialty composition is not chosen to match the mover's own propensity to
refer in-house in each specialty. A mover takes the roster of the practice they join as given, set
by the incumbents and by the system's recruiting rather than by the arriving mover.

Table~\ref{tab:avail} reports the decomposition. The average mover sends 19.3 percent of referrals
in-house while only 5.1 percent of the local choice set is in-house, and the benchmark predicts an
in-house share of 9.1 percent. Among movers who appear in more than one specialty, the actual
in-house share exceeds this benchmark by 10.1 percentage points on average (standard error 0.6). The within-mover
slope of the in-house share on availability is 2.01, well above the null of one, of which distance,
concordance, and quality together account for 1.28 and the residual attributable to practice
affiliation is 0.73 (standard error 0.15), identified from the 3,280 movers who appear in more than
one specialty. The affiliation component is unchanged whether or not specialist quality enters the
benchmark, so the excess does not reflect in-house specialists being more desirable on measured
outcomes. Drawing on variation the specialist-move design does not use, this design points to the
same conclusion, that the pull toward in-house specialists reflects the practice tie rather than
proximity, concordance, or quality.


\subsection{Coordination Value}
\label{sec:coordination}

The practice-affiliation effect is the largest part of what we term convenience, but a shared practice need not be pure convenience to the PCP. It also lets a PCP coordinate the rest of a patient's care within a single organization, and to the extent that coordination benefits the patient, our estimates would absorb it into the affiliation effect. If the affiliation effect reflected coordination value of this kind, it should be larger in practices that also house the specialties that manage a patient's other conditions. We identify those specialties for each focal specialty from clinical co-management together with the specialties that most share patients with it in the Medicare data, keeping cross-domain partners and excluding the focal specialty's own diagnostic and surgical subspecialties.\footnote{For cardiology these are nephrology, endocrinology, pulmonary disease, and vascular surgery; for dermatology, rheumatology, hematology-oncology, and medical oncology; for orthopedic surgery, physical medicine and rehabilitation and pain management.}

We interact the practice-affiliation term with the number of such specialties in the PCP's practice, in a conditional logit for referral formation conditioning on the PCP and year. The interaction is statistically insignificant in all three specialties, suggesting that same-practice effects are no stronger in practices where a larger, related set of the patient's care can be more easily coordinated. This is consistent with the affiliation effect reflecting the convenience of referring within one's own practice rather than a coordination benefit to the patient, and it accords with the utilization results in Section~\ref{sec:welfare}, where convenience-driven ties direct patients toward higher-spending specialists with no offsetting improvement in outcomes.


\section{Peer Referrals and Practice Affiliation}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

Practice affiliation is the dominant predictor of referral formation across all three specialties, but this finding leaves open the question of how organizational ties translate into referral decisions. One possibility is that shared practice membership directly constrains referral choices through internal policies, shared electronic health records, or co-located offices. Another is that newly arrived PCPs learn about local specialists from their practice colleagues and adopt their colleagues' referral patterns, similar to \citet{chan2021} in his study of treatment decisions and seniority in residency programs. To distinguish these possibilities, we construct a measure of peer referral behavior and examine whether it accounts for part of the practice affiliation effect.

For each mover, we measure the pre-arrival referral patterns of incumbent PCPs in the mover's new practice. Specifically, for each specialist in the mover's choice set, we count how many other PCPs in the same practice group referred to that specialist in the year before the mover arrived. This lagged measure captures the practice's established referral network at the time the mover enters, avoiding contamination from the mover's own referral decisions.

Table~\ref{tab:peer} presents the results across all three specialties. We estimate a conditional logit with PCP and year fixed effects and the full-sample specialist fixed effect as an offset, and we report referral-probability-weighted marginal effects on the same scale as our main estimates. Because this is a different estimator from the Jochmans two-stage procedure behind Table~\ref{tab:logit-twfe}, the practice-affiliation effects here are not identical to those in the main table. For each specialty, column (1) is the baseline without peer referrals, column (2) adds the peer referral measure alongside practice affiliation, and column (3) drops practice affiliation entirely. Adding peer referrals reduces the practice-affiliation effect only modestly, from 36.9 to 32.8 percentage points in orthopedic surgery, from 34.1 to 27.7 in cardiology, and from 42.6 to 34.6 in dermatology, a reduction of about a tenth to a fifth. The peer referral variable is positive and significant in all specifications, and its coefficient is largely unchanged when practice affiliation is dropped, indicating that the two variables capture overlapping but not identical variation.

These results indicate that only a modest share of the practice affiliation effect operates through the referral behavior of incumbent colleagues, while most of it persists after conditioning on both peer referrals and the specialist fixed effect, pointing to organizational structure itself as the dominant channel. We caution that peer referral patterns and organizational structure are not easily separable. If a practice has policies or norms that channel referrals to specific specialists, then all PCPs in the practice will exhibit similar referral patterns, and the peer referral variable will absorb what is fundamentally an organizational effect. The residual practice affiliation coefficient in column (2) captures the portion of organizational influence that operates beyond peer referral patterns, and the peer referral coefficient itself reflects a combination of information transmission and shared organizational channeling.

\section{Implications of Convenience-Driven Referrals for Patient Care}
\label{sec:welfare}

Our results indicate that practice affiliation is the dominant driver of referral formation, raising a natural question for policy. Do convenience-driven referral patterns come at a cost to patient care? If PCPs form referral ties on the basis of organizational proximity rather than clinical quality, the resulting allocation of patients across specialists may be inefficient, along the lines of the allocative inefficiency documented in \cite{chandra2020qje} for hospital-level treatment decisions.

To assess this, we summarize each specialist's risk-adjusted outcomes from the two-year outcomes of their Medicare patients, namely spending, non-elective admissions, and mortality. The construction of these risk-adjusted outcomes is presented in the supplemental appendix. For each referral determinant we set its estimated coefficient to zero, so that the determinant no longer shapes referral formation, recompute the model-implied referral probabilities, and compute the change in the probability-weighted risk-adjusted outcomes of a PCP's referrals. A positive value indicates that the determinant directs referrals toward specialists with higher risk-adjusted spending, admissions, or mortality, so that removing it would lower the corresponding outcome. This exercise parallels earlier work that attaches performance measures to observed physician networks \citep{barnett2012mc, landon_using_2013, zeltzer2020}. Because practice affiliation can operate only where a PCP has a same-practice specialist available, we report its effect among those PCPs.

Table~\ref{tab:realloc} reports the estimated reallocation effect of each determinant.\footnote{Confidence intervals are computed by a parametric bootstrap that draws the referral coefficients from their HRR-clustered sampling distribution and each specialist's risk-adjusted outcomes from their empirical-Bayes posterior, recomputing each effect over 400 draws and taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.} We scale each specialty's effects to the referral volume observed among its movers, roughly 10,000 in cardiology and dermatology and 2,000 in orthopedic surgery.\footnote{Among the PCP movers in our sample, we observe 2,070 referrals in orthopedic surgery, 12,004 in cardiology, and 10,633 in dermatology.} In dermatology, practice affiliation is associated with about \$2.4 million in additional risk-adjusted two-year spending and 41 more non-elective admissions per 10,000 referrals (95\% CIs \$1.8 to \$3.0 million and 30 to 55). In cardiology it is associated with 43 more non-elective admissions per 10,000 referrals (95\% CI 28 to 58) and about \$0.5 million in additional spending (95\% CI \$0.2 to \$0.9 million). In orthopedic surgery, practice affiliation has no detectable effect on spending or non-elective admissions. A preference for proximate specialists is associated with higher risk-adjusted spending in cardiology and dermatology (\$0.6 and \$0.8 million per 10,000 referrals, 95\% CIs \$0.4 to \$0.8 million and \$0.6 to \$1.0 million) and lower spending in orthopedic surgery (\$0.21 million per 2,000 referrals, 95\% CI \$0.06 to \$0.31 million). Proximity is also associated with more non-elective admissions in dermatology and cardiology (25 and 12 per 10,000 referrals, 95\% CIs 20 to 30 and 7 to 16) and no detectable effect in orthopedic surgery.

By contrast, concordance in gender, race, age, and experience produces effects that are economically negligible in all three specialties, orders of magnitude smaller than those for organizational affiliation and geographic proximity and amounting at most to a fraction of an admission per referral volume. The utilization consequences of convenience-driven referral formation therefore operate through organizational affiliation and geographic proximity rather than through demographic homophily. Mortality effects are small, with confidence intervals that generally include zero, so the additional spending and admissions associated with these ties do not correspond to a consistent difference in survival.

Referrals within a shared practice may also carry a convenience benefit for the patient, since care coordinated within a single practice can be easier to schedule and manage than care split across unaffiliated offices. But the affiliation effect is no stronger where the practice can better coordinate a patient's broader care (Section~\ref{sec:coordination}), and in any case these ties do not come with more efficient use of health care resources, since in cardiology and dermatology they direct patients toward specialists with higher rather than lower risk-adjusted spending and admissions.

These estimates measure the risk-adjusted outcomes of the specialists to whom convenience directs referrals, not the outcome a particular patient would have experienced under a different specialist. Because we observe each specialist's average risk-adjusted outcomes rather than the patient-specific effect of specialist choice, we read the associated spending and admission differences as the outcome composition of convenience-driven referrals for the average patient rather than as the causal effect of any single referral. The determinants appear to operate differently across the three clinical settings, most visibly in the muted effects for orthopedic surgery. It is also where practice affiliation attenuates over time, and the muted pattern is consistent with PCPs leaning less on convenience where a specialist's contribution is likely more observable than in our dermatology or cardiology referral settings. Identifying the mechanisms behind these differences would require comparing a broader range of clinical settings, which we leave to future work.

Because the initial network is durable, these consequences are not confined to the year of formation. The persistence documented in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} implies that a meaningful share of the ties a PCP forms on arrival continues to route patients in later years, so the reliance on organizational and geographic convenience at entry exposes patients to a portion of these costs well beyond the first year, even as the pull of the initial network fades.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In forming referral networks, experts can rely on convenience, such as organizational affiliation and geographic proximity, and the effects of these factors can persist and carry meaningful implications for how services are delivered. We study three clinical areas with distinct institutional settings, namely orthopedic surgery, cardiology, and dermatology, using the 100\% Medicare FFS claims data from 2010 through 2018. Our identification strategy exploits PCP movers who relocate to a new market and must build their referral networks from scratch. We employ the quadruple-based estimator of \cite{jochmans2018}, which allows for both PCP and specialist fixed effects, together with a second-stage fixed-effect recovery procedure that enables marginal-effect calculations on the probability scale.

Across all three specialties, practice group affiliation is by far the largest contributor to initial link formation, with geographic proximity also emerging as a robust and persistent factor. Gender concordance is meaningful in orthopedic surgery but plays a smaller role in dermatology and is negligible for cardiology. Other measures of demographic concordance, including age, experience, and race, play at best a limited role. As PCPs spend more time in their new market, the effect of practice affiliation attenuates in orthopedic surgery but not in cardiology or dermatology. Link decomposition and period-specific estimation confirm that the orthopedic decline reflects genuine behavioral change rather than compositional dilution, consistent with organizational convenience playing a diminishing role where a specialist's contribution is most observable to the referring PCP.

The gender-concordance dimension of our results connects to \citet{zeltzer2020}, who documents robust gender homophily in Medicare referrals using a related discrete-choice framework. We also incorporate gender alongside practice affiliation and geographic proximity, and we identify these determinants using a mover-based design where PCPs build their referral networks from scratch after relocating, together with a two-sided fixed-effects estimator for PCPs and specialists. By examining three specialties with distinct institutional settings and demographic compositions, we show that the dominance of organizational ties in referral formation is not specific to any single clinical area, though the magnitude varies across specialties.

We note that the practice-affiliation effect bundles two forces we do not separate. A shared
practice lowers the cost of referring in-house, and a practice can also actively channel referrals
inward through how it structures coordination, scheduling, and the handling of outside referrals
\citep{whaley2024-jpube, singh2023}. For where patients end up the distinction does not matter,
since either force routes the referral through organizational structure rather than a specialist's
match quality. Neither appears to reflect a coordination benefit to the patient, since the affiliation
effect is no stronger in practices where a larger, related set of the patient's care can be more
easily coordinated (Section~\ref{sec:coordination}).

We conclude with three policy observations. First, while increasing horizontal integration of physician practices can have large negative consequences for competitiveness and physician pricing \citep{koch2021}, our results point to a further channel. Insofar as mergers strengthen the practice affiliations that shape referrals, they can direct patients toward specialists with higher risk-adjusted spending and more non-elective admissions, without a detectable difference in survival.
Second, our results highlight the role of geographic proximity as a persistent factor in referral formation. Because referral choices are effectively constrained to a narrow geographic subset of specialists, demographic homophily has limited scope to affect patient outcomes. Whether the same would hold in settings where distance is less binding (e.g., telehealth or urban specialist density) is an open question for future work. Third, while organizational affiliation plays a dominant role in PCPs' initial referral networks, these effects attenuate over time in orthopedic surgery, the setting where specialist quality is most observable, though not in cardiology or dermatology. This attenuation suggests that the initial convenience-driven network is not permanent and that PCPs adjust their referral patterns as they gain local experience.

Taken together, convenience is the dominant factor in referral formation. In cardiology and dermatology, the specialists chosen for convenience have higher risk-adjusted spending and more non-elective admissions, with no detectable difference in survival. Because these referral relationships persist, an allocation shaped by organizational and geographic convenience continues to direct patients for years, leaving a lasting mark on health care spending and utilization.
